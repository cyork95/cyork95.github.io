Lesson 1 was about laying out your ingredients. This one is about the ingredient people reach for last and should reach for first: an example.

When you describe what you want, you almost always reach for adjectives — *professional, witty, concise, warm, punchy*. The problem is that adjectives are promises, not specifications. "Professional" to you might mean crisp and understated; the model averages it across every business document it has ever seen and hands you corporate oatmeal. "Funnier" is worse — funnier than what, in whose style, aimed at whom? Every adjective is a small act of interpretation, and the model does the interpreting without you in the room.

An example skips the interpretation entirely. Paste one piece of writing you like and you've transmitted tone, length, structure, vocabulary, and rhythm in a single block — including things you couldn't have described if you tried. Nobody can write "make it sound like this" as an adjective list. With the example on the counter, you don't have to.

There's a second benefit: an example sets both the ceiling and the floor. It shows the model the standard to hit *and* the register to stay inside. And it works in reverse, too — a marked bad example ("not like this — too salesy") fences off the failure mode you're actually worried about, which no positive adjective ever quite manages.

The working rule: **whenever you catch yourself stacking a third adjective, stop and find an example instead.**

### Before / after

❌ **Telling**

> Write a product announcement for our new dark mode feature. Make it exciting, professional but fun, concise, and really engaging.

Five adjectives, zero information. The model will produce the average of every product announcement ever written: "We're thrilled to announce…" You can picture it because you've read it a hundred times — that's exactly the problem.

✅ **Showing**

> Write a product announcement for our new dark mode feature, matching this announcement we sent last spring:
>
> *"Night owls, rejoice: offline mode is here. Your notes now sync when you're back — no buttons, no babysitting. It's on every plan, starting today."*
>
> Same length, same structure, same tone. New details: dark mode ships today, the toggle is in Settings → Appearance, and it's on all plans.

The example carries the tone, the rhythm, and the shape. The prompt only has to supply what's *different* — which turns out to be three facts. That's the whole job.

### Try it

Find a prompt where you've been piling up adjectives — "make it more X, a bit Y, kind of Z." Delete the adjectives. Paste in one example of output you genuinely liked (yours, a colleague's, anyone's), add one line — "match this style; change only the topic" — and re-run.

Compare the two answers. The adjectives told the model what you *hoped* for. The example showed it what you *meant*.
